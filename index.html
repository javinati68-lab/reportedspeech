1️⃣ How Reported Speech Works Across Sentence Types & Reporting Verbs

When we report what someone said without quoting their exact words, we remove quotation marks (" "), adjust pronouns, shift verb tenses one step into the past (backshifting), and update time/place expressions. We also choose accurate reporting verbs such as said, told, asked, warned, advised, suggested, promised, denied, explained, and reminded.

Affirmative Statements

Direct: "I work at the hospital," Elena said.

Reported: Elena said (that) she worked at the hospital.

Direct: "I will help you tomorrow," Leo told me.

Reported: Leo promised to help me the next day.

Negative Statements

Direct: "We don't like cold weather," they said.

Reported: They explained (that) they didn't like cold weather.

Direct: "I didn't steal the keys," he said.

Reported: He denied stealing the keys.

Interrogative (Questions — No Question Mark!)

Yes/No Direct: "Are you ready?" she asked me.

Reported: She asked me if/whether I was ready.

Wh- Direct: "Where do you live?" he asked.

Reported: He asked where I lived. (Statement word order)

Commands, Advice & Suggestions

Command: "Open your books," the teacher told us.

Reported: The teacher told us to open our books.

Negative Warning: "Don't run!" she warned him.

Reported: She warned him not to run. / He suggested going home.

The Real Sherlock Holmes

Before Sherlock Holmes became the most famous detective in fiction, his creator, Arthur Conan Doyle, studied medicine in Edinburgh under Dr Joseph Bell. Bell was a respected surgeon who treated observation as a discipline. He told his students that they should notice details before forming theories, and he warned them that a careless glance could hide the most important clue.

Doyle later recalled that Bell could examine a stranger and announce where the person had worked or travelled. According to one famous account, Bell asked a patient whether he had recently served in a Highland regiment, and the man admitted that he had. Bell then explained that the patient had kept his hat on, a habit of military life, and that his skin complaint was common in the West Indies. Retellings differ, however, and historians suspect that some details were improved over the years.

Bell insisted that his method was not magic. He suggested that anyone could learn it through practice, and he reminded students that every conclusion must be tested against further evidence. Doyle acknowledged that Bell had inspired Holmes, yet Bell modestly denied deserving the credit and claimed that Doyle had created the character himself.

Today, scholars still debate how closely the fictional detective resembled his real-life model. Nevertheless, the lesson attributed to Bell remains useful: strong conclusions begin with careful observation, and confident explanations must always depend on evidence rather than assumption.
